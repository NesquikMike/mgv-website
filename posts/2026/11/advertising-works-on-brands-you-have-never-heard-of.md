title: Advertising Works on Brands You Have Never Heard Of

There is a familiar finding about advertising. You take two similar neighbouring districts. You show an advert in one and not the other. Sales barely move. A [Freakonomics Radio episode](https://freakonomics.com/podcast/does-advertising-actually-work-part-1-tv-ep-440/){target="_blank" rel="noopener"} walked through this kind of border design. Television markets sit next to each other. The people on each side of the line look alike. The ads they see do not. For a lot of established brands, doubling spend is associated with a tiny change in sales. In a related digital experiment, turning off eBay's paid search barely dented purchases. The brand was already in people's heads.

That result gets recited as "advertising does not work." I do not think that is what it says. It says advertising does not work *as a sales lever for a brand you already know*.

Now look at the early 2020s, when fast grocery apps arrived in London as if they had been invented that morning. Gorillas. Getir. Weezy. A year earlier most people I knew could not have named them. Then the bikes were everywhere, and the bus stops were everywhere, and the name was in your mouth before you had ever opened the app. You can see the awareness jump in [Google Trends](https://trends.google.com/){target="_blank" rel="noopener"}. That is not a border study. It is still a clue. Something took a set of unknown brands and made them known, fast. The obvious something is advertising, plus the bikes themselves as moving billboards.

My hypothesis is crude and I think it is mostly right. For a new brand, the job of an advert is not to squeeze a 1% lift out of existing demand. The job is to create a slot in memory. Once the slot exists, habit and availability can do the rest. For an established brand, the slot already exists. Extra ads do not create it. They mostly remind you of a name you were going to buy anyway, or would never buy.

Why, then, do the established brands keep buying the space?

Because the space is finite. If Unilever and the supermarket own brand and the incumbent delivery app fill the billboards, the next Gorillas has nowhere cheap to stand. The campaign that "does not shift the dial" on this quarter's volume can still be rational. It is rent on a barrier. You are not buying sales. You are buying the absence of a new name in the customer's week.

This is why the neighbouring-district studies and the delivery-app explosion can both be true. The studies are usually about brands that already occupy the slot. The apps were brands that did not. Different job. Different return.

If you work in measurement, this is an annoying conclusion. It means the A/B test on spend, run on an incumbent, will look like a waste, and the procurement team will still be right to renew. You are measuring the wrong outcome. Measure whether a new name can get in.

I would like a cleaner test. Take a city. Hold back a truly unknown brand from advertising in one half of it. Let it buy every surface in the other half. Watch Trends, app installs, and prompted awareness, not just next-week sales. I am betting the two halves will not look like eBay.
