A lot of white-collar work used to look like producing the artefact. The slide deck. The SQL. The legal memo. The ticket. That is still what goes in the folder. It is less and less what the human is for.

What the human is for, more and more, is context. You spend the morning stuffing a model with the parts of the world it cannot see. The names of the stakeholders. The constraint that legal will not accept. The metric that looks good and is wrong. The git history of the last three failed attempts. Then you look at what comes back, throw away the confident nonsense, and go again.

I do this at work. An A/B test on a self-driving stack is not just a randomisation. It is a pile of decisions about who is in the test, what "comfort" means, what counts as a disengagement, and which slice of London is allowed to fool you. The model can help you write the query. It cannot know, unless you tell it, that last quarter's definition quietly excluded the cases you now care about.

I do this on this website. Eleventy will build whatever I give it. The work is remembering that drafts should not ship, that a private repo should not be linked, that the sentence about Obe Ata should not sound like a slur. None of that is in the training data as *my* problem.

The old story of knowledge work was that experts hold facts. The new story is that experts hold the frame. Facts are cheap. Frames are not. A junior can paste an error into a chat box. A senior knows which error is a symptom and which is the disease.

This is why the job feels both easier and more tiring. The typing is easier. The accountability is not. If the model is wrong, you were the person who chose the prompt, the files, the evaluation. You were the context machine.

There is an optimistic version. If thinking from scratch is slow, and copying is fast, then a model is a copying engine with a very large set of examples. Your comparative advantage is pointing it at the right examples and stopping it when it copies the wrong ones. The pessimistic version is that organisations will hire fewer people and ask those people to feed more context into more models, until the job is nothing but glue.

I think the glue *is* the job. The people who are good at it will look, from the outside, like they do not produce much. They produce the conditions under which production is not garbage.

If you want a test, watch what happens when you take the context away. Give the same model to someone who does not know the product. Watch the fluent wrongness. That gap is the remaining human work.
