Peanuts are ordinary. They are in packed lunches and at the bar. They are cheap protein with a long shelf life. And yet, in a lot of the West, they acquired a second life as a source of dread. Schools ban them. Parents swap notes about which plane is safe. The mystery is not that some people are allergic. The mystery is the timing. Peanut foods became more common. Peanut allergy became more common too. That is the wrong direction if you think exposure is what trains the body.

Peanuts are not nuts. They are legumes. They were taken up in the Americas, then carried around the Atlantic world as a crop that grew in poor soil and stored well. In the United States they were pushed hard as a foodstuff in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Peanut butter made them a staple of childhood. Britain got them later as a snack and a sandwich filling rather than as a daily infant food.

For a while official advice in the UK and the US told parents of high-risk infants to delay allergenic foods, including peanut. The idea was caution. Keep the dangerous thing away until the child is older. If that advice had been right, allergy should have fallen, or at least not risen, among the children who avoided peanut. It did not work like that.

A useful clue came from comparing Jewish children in the UK with Jewish children in Israel. Genetically similar groups. Very different peanut habits. In Israel, infants often ate peanut snacks early. In the UK, peanut was delayed. Peanut allergy was much more common in the UK group.

That observation helped produce a different story. The [dual allergen exposure hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_allergy#Causes){target="_blank" rel="noopener"} says the route matters. Eating peanut early, through the mouth, tends to train tolerance. Meeting peanut protein through broken skin, especially eczematous skin, without having eaten it, tends to train allergy. Dust in a house that eats peanut butter is not nothing if a baby has a damaged skin barrier and has never had peanut on a spoon.

The [LEAP trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1414850){target="_blank" rel="noopener"} tested the eating part. High-risk infants were randomised to eat peanut regularly or to avoid it. The eaters had far less peanut allergy by age five. Advice flipped. The mystery looks less like peanuts becoming more poisonous, and more like a generation meeting peanuts in the wrong order.

I like this story because it is a copying story as well as a medical one. Parents copied the cautious guideline. The guideline was a reasoned guess. The guess, applied at scale, may have helped create the thing it was trying to prevent. Conscious thought is fast to adopt and slow to correct. By the time the trial landed, a lot of households had already been trained.

The peanut did not change. The culture around the peanut did.
