title: Gentrification Was Hiding in the Fertility Rate

People talk about gentrification as if it were a taste. One day a borough wanted warehouses. The next day it wanted flat whites. That story is too thin. A lot of what happened to inner London was sitting in the fertility statistics decades earlier.

In the 1960s a typical pattern, if you were young and starting a family in London, was to leave. People in their twenties had children earlier than they do now. As soon as the children arrived they wanted a more familial environment: a garden, quieter streets, other families next door. The London home counties were built for that. Zone 2 was somewhere you passed through on the way out.

Then the age at first birth moved. People spent longer in their twenties and thirties without children. They still wanted a life. They just wanted a different one. A peaceful familial environment is less urgent when there is no family yet. A fast-paced social environment is more valuable when your evenings are still your own.

So the same people who would once have gone to Surrey stayed in Hackney and Notting Hill. They stayed in the noisy, dense, well-connected bits of Zone 2. They paid for restaurants and pubs and parks that work at 11pm. Landlords noticed. Builders noticed. Councils noticed, eventually.

Once you see it this way, the coffee shops are a lagging indicator. The leading indicator is when people have their first child. Delay that by five or ten years and you have created a large class of relatively high-earning adults who want urban social life rather than a lawn. They will bid up the streets that supply that life. The people who used those streets for cheaper housing get squeezed. That is gentrification. It looks cultural. A lot of it is demographic.

This is copying as well as economics. People copy the lives they can see. If the high-status version of your twenties is a warehouse conversion in Hackney, that is what gets imitated. If the high-status version is a semi in the home counties with a pram in the hall, that is what gets imitated instead. Status followed the fertility timetable.

I do not mean that cafes and planning policy did nothing. They did. I mean that a lot of the surprise around inner London should not have been a surprise. You could have watched the age of first birth climb, asked where childless twenty-somethings would want to live, and pointed at Zone 2.

The next time someone tells you a neighbourhood changed because of vibe, ask when people in that city started having children. The vibe is downstream.
